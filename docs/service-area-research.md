# A-1 Drain Cleaning — Service-Area Research Register

This register is the evidence ledger for city-specific service-area pages. It is not customer-facing page copy. A town must not be marked researched merely because its name has been added to the city registry.

## Publishing standard

For each city page, verify and record:
1. Correct city/county assignment using Iowa government records.
2. Housing and development history only where supported by census, city history, assessor, planning, or other reliable records.
3. Public wastewater collection/treatment facts from the city, sanitary district, engineering documents, or Iowa DNR permits/reports.
4. Pipe material only when an authoritative record actually identifies it. Never infer Orangeburg, clay tile, cast iron, or another material solely from a house's age.
5. Soil, landform, groundwater, and flood facts from USDA NRCS, USGS, Iowa DNR, or other authoritative mapping/data.
6. A clear distinction between public sewer infrastructure and the property's private building drain/sewer lateral.
7. Plumbing guidance framed as possibilities, not a diagnosis: heavy rain can coincide with backups, but a particular home's cause requires inspection.
8. A short list of source URLs and the exact claim each source supports.

Do not publish guessed local failure rates, claims that a city's public sewer is overloaded, unsupported pipe-material claims, or claims that local soil causes a particular home's backup. Do not generate city-name-swapped pages. If a town lacks enough verified information for genuinely distinct copy, keep it out of the developed-page directory until research is complete.

## Initial research: Clear Lake, Iowa (Cerro Gordo County)

### Verified facts suitable for cautious page context
- The Clear Lake Sanitary District's history records that the district's sanitary sewer system began operating on September 17, 1956. The history says the initial system included more than 12 miles of main lines and five pumping stations; this is a historical snapshot, not a description of today's system.
- The district's current treatment page says its collection system serves more than 5,000 homes and businesses, with about 60 miles of pipe and 11 pump stations.
- The district's history documents a June 1998 torrential downpour and pump-station problems. It also documents a sump-pump inspection program begun in 2009 because stormwater entering sanitary sewers can consume capacity and contribute to overloads and basement backups.
- The district explicitly distinguishes stormwater systems from sanitary sewer systems. This supports practical homeowner guidance about not directing sump-pump discharge into sanitary sewer piping; it does not prove any particular home has an illegal connection or that a current backup is caused by rain.
- The district documents cured-in-place lining of a deep gravity sewer and rehabilitation of a 1950s force-main section. These are public-system projects; they do not establish the pipe material or condition of private home laterals.

### Primary sources
- Clear Lake Sanitary District history: https://clearlakesd.org/history/
  Supports the system start date, historical line/station counts, documented downpour/pump-station event, and sump-pump inspection program.
- Clear Lake Sanitary District treatment overview: https://clearlakesd.org/treatment/
  Supports current published collection-system scale and pump-station count.
- Clear Lake Sanitary District FAQ: https://clearlakesd.org/faq/
  Supports district service area and the distinction between the sanitary district and city governments.
- Clear Lake Sanitary District technology: https://clearlakesd.org/technology/
  Supports the district's documented public-system lining and force-main work.
- USDA NRCS Web Soil Survey: https://websoilsurvey.nrcs.usda.gov/app/
  Use for parcel- or area-specific soil context only after checking the actual map unit. Do not make generalized claims about every Clear Lake property.
- Iowa DNR floodplain mapping: https://www.iowadnr.gov/environmental-protection/land-quality/flood-plain-management/floodplain-mapping
  Use for mapped flood-hazard context only; mapped riverine flood hazard is not the same thing as a sanitary-sewer backup diagnosis.

### Page-copy guardrails
Appropriate: “Clear Lake's public sanitary system has documented decades of collection-system upgrades, and the district has documented how stormwater entering sanitary sewers can reduce available capacity during wet weather. A wet-weather backup at a home still needs its own diagnosis; the cause may be in the private lateral, a building drain, or elsewhere.”
Not appropriate: “Clear Lake homes have Orangeburg pipes,” “the city sewer is overloaded,” or “rain causes your backup.” None of those claims is established for an individual property by the cited sources.

- The City of Northwood's Public Works page says the Northwood Wastewater Treatment Facility was constructed in 2018 and uses an activated-sludge process.
- This establishes a treatment-facility date and process. It does not establish the installation date, material, or condition of sewer mains or private laterals throughout Northwood.

## Initial research: Garner, Iowa (Hancock County)

### Verified facts suitable for cautious page context
- Garner's Public Works department identifies separate Streets/Storm Sewer and Water/Sewer units. This supports explaining that storm drainage and sanitary sewer service are distinct municipal functions, but it does not show that a specific property has cross-connected systems.
- A city-hosted retrospective reports wastewater treatment plant/trunkline improvements totaling $5 million by the end of 2011. Treat this as a historical summary, not a current condition report.
- The city advertises more than 140 new homes built since 1998. This indicates that the housing stock includes newer development as well as older properties; it does not establish the age or pipe material at a particular address.

### Primary sources
- Garner Public Works: https://garneriowa.org/departments/public-works/
- Garner community retrospective (PDF): https://www.garneriowa.org/vertical/sites/%7B6C000A23-F8A6-4C5C-8FC6-CCE607846EED%7D/uploads/10_years_of_accomplishments.PDF
- Garner city home/residential development: https://garneriowa.org/

### Research still required
Find city/county historic housing and collection-system records, plus authoritative local soil and floodplain context. Do not infer Orangeburg or other pipe materials from housing age alone.

## Initial research: Charles City, Iowa (Floyd County)

### Verified facts suitable for cautious page context
- Charles City Area Development Corporation says much of the city's physical infrastructure was rebuilt after the 1968 tornado. Its statement is broad and does not establish which sewer segments were replaced, what materials were installed, or the condition of present-day private laterals.
- The same source refers to a recently completed water-quality plant; confirm with City of Charles City primary records before using this as wastewater-treatment history, because “water quality plant” may refer to drinking water rather than wastewater.

### Primary source
- Charles City Area Development Corporation, infrastructure overview: https://www.charlescityia.com/s-projects-side-by-side

### Research still required
Obtain a City of Charles City public-works/wastewater source, clarify whether any 1968 rebuilding records include sanitary sewer assets, and research local flood/soil and housing history. Do not imply the tornado replaced sewer lines without documentation.
